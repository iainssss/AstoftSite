Wells Cathedral Architecture - Interior

Notes in italics from North Somerset and Bristol by Nikolaus Pevsner  (1958) Yale University Press, New Haven and London. See the book for more extensive description.		  
         
         
                   
         
  North Transept (north-east, east and west). We can safely go to the TRANSEPTS for an impression of the whole of the (original) design of  Wells in c. 1185-1200. ... The piers (in the chancel and here) consist of a solid core of Greek cross shape and attached to it twenty-four shafts in groups of three in trefoil grouping, three to each main direction, three to each diagonal. The abaci  are square over the diagonals, polygonal in the main direction, three to each diagonal. Stiff leaf capitals. The arches have a variety of roll mouldings ...
Above the arcade in an English cathedral of the C12 or C13 one would expect a gallery. At Wells there is a triforium instead ... grouped into two twin openings per bay separated from the next by the vaulting shafts, which start, not on the capitals below, but on corbels above the triforium sill. So the sill is drawn as one uninterrupted horizontal. The triforium openings have continuous mouldings (a roll and a chamfer), a favourite motif of Wells ... which can be traced back to the Late Norman of e.g. Bristol.
The next details to be observed refers to the end walls of the two transepts. They are identical inside, though, as we have see, they differ outside. There are windows below, separated by triple shafts, and windows above with depressed two-centred rere-arches. But between the two zones the triforium runs here as a blank  frieze of five arches, not divided into groups. This must be stressed; for it contributes the main innovation of the nave and the most memorable feature of the Wells interior.  		 
         
                   
         
  The nave is in its interior completely of Bishop Jocelyn's time (1206-1242), and it is rightly taken as Early English Wells par excellence. ... (The crossing tower with its strainer arches is later, and described below).
The nave is ten bays long. Piers, aisle shafts, aisle vaults, high vaults are as in the transepts (except that the piers now have the subtle touch of keeling the shafts pointing to the diagonals ... ). But the great innovation is this. The grouping of the triforium in twins is given up, and it runs through from E to W as one seemingly interminable band. To achieve that, the designer pushed up the corbels for the vaulting shafts to just below the clerestory level. That removes one more interference of the vertical with the horizontal. ...
Wells is the best place in England to enjoy and study stiff-leaf capitals. Stiff-leaf foliage was an English speciality anyway; C13  capitals of stylized foliage as beautiful as those of Wells and Lincoln do not exist anywhere outside England. They make the crocket capitals of France look dull. Wells presents us with the whole gamut of stiff-leaf ...		  
     
              
         
  Nave aisles, south and north, looking west.  
         
                   
         
  West End of the Nave. It is clearly designed to a different scheme. The doorway has a depressed two-centred arch and is flanked by trefoiled blank arcading. Purbeck shafts are again used as they are so  excessively outside. ... The zone of the big W windows was reshaped in the Perpendicular period. The horizontal ribs are patent, also above, where the Perp capitals carry Early English arches with dog-tooth decoration. Stiff-leaf also reappears in the top spandrels.  
         
              
         
  The Crossing Tower of 1315-22 had to be strengthened very soon after its completion. The way in which this was done is sensational ... The strainer arches are huge, and they are - according to how one looks at them - two intersected ogee curves or an arch standing on its head on a normal arch. ... The arches rise straight from the ground, without responds to carry them, and a thin triple-chamfer with quadrant moulding is carried on all up the crossing piers.
A tower can be shored up in other ways. That this is the way chosen here shows once again, with no punches pulled, what the Decorated style is concerned with in England in matters of space. No smooth vista along the nave and up into the crossing tower. Let it be filtered through these gargantuan meshes. The obstacle to the eye is worth more than the vista.
The Perpendicular style added almost nothing to Wells except pieces of furnishing. The only architectural contribution is the fan-vault in the crossing tower which dates from c.1480.		  
         
         
              
         
  The Chancel, looking west (first two pictures). It is of two periods, the western-most part retains some of its Early English structure of around 1200, the nearer eastern part is of the 1330s in the Decorated period. In the latter: Principal shafts of Purbeck marble with foliage capitals ... A continuous moulding runs up all the way to the  vault and frames arcade bay and clerestory bay together ... But the memorable  feature of the Wells chancel is the grille of delicate stone forms between arcade and clerestory ... As far as possible the same grille was laid over the early C13 upper wall further W.
The vault is a tour de force. Though  it keeps transverse arches, it has no intention any longer of stressing bays. There are e.g. no diagonal ribs, only diagonal ribs tying together two bays at a time. There are also no ridge-ribs, and instead a lierne pattern crystallized in squares at the ends of a saltire cross - a rectangular, wholly arbitrary pattern. The squares incidentally are cusped ... No bosses, but leaf sprays at the junctions.
Chancel aisles in last two pictures, first is north aisle looking west, second is south aisle looking east. The vaults are lierne stars of yet a different pattern (without any diagonal ribs or ridge-ribs) and they were substituted for the original, simple, quadripartite vaults in the W bays also (seen in last picture, along with the older pier of the earlier part of the chancel). There are again no real bosses but rather sprays of foliage, where the ribs meet.  		 
         
              
         
  The Lady Chapel was begun at an unknown date ... There is reason to assume that  it was complete by  1319.
An apse of three sides ... Trefoiled wall shafts with knobbly capitals ... The motif of the window tracery is arches upon arches - a kind of pre-reticulation ... Fleuron band below the windows. The S doorway and the Sedilia are of excellent quality, with ogee arches, crockets and finials. It deserves very special mention that the Sedilia have nodding or three-dimenional ogee arches; for unless the Sedilia are an addition, made when the chapel was ready, they are amongst the earliest  nodding ogee arches on record. ... The vault has the first liernes of Wells ... They are made to appear a  star within a star. From each shaft rise diagonal ribs and three pairs of tiercerons. The inner star is formed by the third tiercerons and not by liernes. Bosses and sprays of knobbly foliage at the rib junctions. ... 		 
         
              
         
  The Retrochoir with its chapels l. and r. of the Lady Chapel ... must have gone up at about the same time as the Lady Chapel. ... There are  six clustered shafts in the retrochoir plus the two intermediate piers at the E end of the chancel  itself (last picture). ...
